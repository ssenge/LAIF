# A randomized classical algorithm for exponential congruences

Written 25 Nov 2026.

For a, b, c, f, g ∈ F_q^* with f and g of multiplicative orders s and t, the task is to find integers x, y ≥ 0 with

a f^x + b g^y = c,

or to decide that none exist. The deterministic algorithm of [1] runs in time q^{9/8+o(1)}, and the baby-step giant-step algorithm of [2] in time min{(st)^{1/2}, q^{3/4}} (log q)^{O(1)}. Quantum algorithms run in time q^{3/8} (log q)^{O(1)} for all orders [1, Theorem 3] and q^{1/2} (st)^{−1/4} (log q)^{O(1)} when st > C q^{3/2} (log q)^{1/2} [1, Theorem 4]. On the role of randomness, [1] notes that subexponential algorithms for discrete logarithms exist for some fields, while "in general probabilistic algorithms do not seem to give any significant advantage for our problem". Compared with the deterministic bounds, [3, Section 2.5] lists a sixth-power gap at s, t ≈ q^{3/4} (q^{3/4} against q^{1/8}) and a fourth-power gap for typical c at s, t ≈ q^{2/3}.

**Proposition 1.** Let s ≥ t and st ≥ C q^{3/2} log q, with C the absolute constant of [1, Lemma 1]. The following Las Vegas algorithm finds a solution in expected time (q/s) · L, with L the cost of one discrete logarithm in F_q^* plus (log q)^{O(1)}:

1. Draw y uniformly from {0, …, t − 1} and put w = a^{−1}(c − b g^y).
2. If w^s = 1, output y and x = log_f w. Otherwise repeat.

*Proof.* Every y has at most one x ∈ {0, …, s − 1} with a f^x + b g^y = c, and w lies in the group generated by f exactly when w^s = 1. A draw therefore succeeds with probability N/t, where N is the number of solutions with x < s and y < t. By [1, Lemma 1] with r = t, N = st/(q − 1) + O(q^{1/2} log q) ≥ st/(2q) in the stated range, so the expected number of draws is at most 2q/s. Each draw costs (log q)^{O(1)}, and the logarithm is computed once. ∎

Index calculus computes discrete logarithms in F_q^* in subexponential time, rigorously L_q[1/2] for prime q and heuristically L_q[1/3] for every q, so L = q^{o(1)}. In the range of [1, Theorem 4] the classical time is then q^{1+o(1)}/max(s, t), against the quantum q^{1/2} max(s, t)^{−1/2} (log q)^{O(1)} of the proof of that theorem. The gap is quadratic. At s, t ≈ q^{3/4} this is q^{1/4+o(1)} against q^{1/8}. For orders below that range, applying step 1 to every y ∈ {0, …, min(s, t) − 1}, with the roles of f and g swapped when t > s, takes min(s, t) · q^{o(1)} against the quantum min(s, t)^{1/2} (log q)^{O(1)}, which is also quadratic. With black-box discrete logarithms in place of index calculus, step 2 costs s^{1/2} and the comparison of [1] applies.

## Measurements

[`sim/sampling.py`](sim/sampling.py) checks, by exhaustive computation over prime fields with p ≤ 4001 (717 triples (p, s, t) with s ≥ t and st ≥ p, random a, b, c, f, g):

- the number N of solutions equals the number of y with a^{−1}(c − b g^y) in the group generated by f, so a draw succeeds with probability N/t;
- |N − st/(p − 1)| ≤ p^{1/2} ln p, the error term of [1, Lemma 1] with constant 1 at these sizes;
- every output of the sampler is a solution, and the mean number of draws over 200 runs lies within [0.6, 1.6] · t/N.

Run it with `python sampling.py` in `sim/`.

## Limits

- The algorithm is randomized, and the bound uses subexponential discrete logarithms. For deterministic algorithms the bounds of [1] and [2] stand.
- The proposition covers finding a solution in the range where [1, Lemma 1] guarantees many. Deciding that no solution exists is covered only by the enumeration over min(s, t) values of y.
- Not formalized in Lean.
